The bank [[ivy-helen-norman-1899|Ivy Norman]] worked at from 1913 to 1921 was properly called **London County and Westminster Bank** during most of her time there. The name *Westminster Bank* was only adopted in 1923. The institution traced its origins to the London & Westminster Bank (founded 1834, initially at 38 Throgmorton Street, moving to 41 Lothbury in 1838). A series of mergers produced the version Ivy knew:

- **1909:** London & Westminster Bank merged with London and County Bank to form London County and Westminster Bank
- **1917:** Acquired Ulster Bank (170 branches)
- **1918:** Merged with Parr's Bank to form London County Westminster & Parr's Bank Limited — by this point the fifth-largest bank in England, with around 700 branches and a balance sheet of £286 million
- **1923:** Name shortened to Westminster Bank Ltd
- **1970:** Merged with National Provincial Bank to form National Westminster Bank (NatWest)

## Women and the Wartime Bank

When Ivy joined in 1913, women made up under 1% of bank staff across the industry. The outbreak of war in August 1914 changed this rapidly: as men enlisted, banks took on women in large numbers, first in back-office roles (voucher-sorting, filing) and then, as the labour shortage deepened, at the teller counter itself. By 1921 women accounted for around 25% of banking staff nationally.

Ivy was part of an early cohort — she started just before the wartime wave, at a moment when female tellers were still unusual.

Working conditions were strictly segregated. Women were typically housed in separate office spaces, were not to speak to male colleagues in passageways, and were sent home before 9pm. Pay was more than a boy apprentice but well below any experienced male clerk.

## The Work

All entries were made by hand in bound ledger books. Tellers worked in pre-decimal currency (pounds, shillings, pence), requiring three-column arithmetic with non-decimal carries. Adding machines were only beginning to appear; mental arithmetic was the standard tool. Tellers could not leave at the end of the day until their books balanced — the 3pm close (1pm Saturday) was the public deadline, but staff stayed until reconciled.

## The Marriage Bar

Women employed by the bank were required to leave upon marriage. This was immediate and non-negotiable. Ivy left in June 1921 when she married Roland Bunker. The marriage bar persisted in UK banking until the 1950s — Lloyds abolished it in 1949, Barclays not until 1961.
