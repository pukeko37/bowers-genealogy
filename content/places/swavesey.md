Swavesey is a village in the South Cambridgeshire fenlands, roughly eight miles north-west of Cambridge. Fen Stanton (also written Fenstanton) lies five miles to its north-east, just across the county boundary in Huntingdonshire. The two villages, together with their neighbours Over, Fen Drayton, and Elsworth, formed a tightly connected agricultural community straddling the Cambridgeshire/Huntingdonshire border — the home territory of Charles Norman's paternal and maternal roots for at least three generations.

## The county boundary problem

Victorian census records for this cluster of villages alternate confusingly between "Cambridgeshire" and "Huntingdonshire." Swavesey is geographically in Cambridgeshire, but for most of the 19th century it fell within the **St Ives registration district**, which was administered from Huntingdonshire. This is why so many FamilySearch records give the same village as both "Swavesey, Huntingdonshire, England" (1851, 1861, 1871, 1881) and "Swavesey, Cambridgeshire, England" (1841, 1891 onward). All registrations of Norman births in Swavesey appear at **St Ives** — the district office. County identity was genuinely ambiguous here, and the boundary was redrawn more than once during the 19th century.

## The Tabraham/Eusden presence: Elsworth and Fen Stanton

The first family rooted in this area in the record is the Tabraham family of **Elsworth**, Cambridgeshire, and **Fen Stanton**, Huntingdonshire.

**[[richard-tabraham-1807|Richard Tabraham]]** (1807–1874) was christened in Elsworth on 1 February 1807 and died there on 26 February 1874 — a man who rarely if ever left the parish of his birth. His address through the 1841, 1851, and 1871 censuses was **Bell Lane, Fenstanton** — the specific address gives the feel of a man embedded in his village. He was an agricultural worker in an agricultural landscape: the flat fen-edge country between Cambridge and St Ives offered little other livelihood.

**[[mary-eusden-1807|Mary Eusden]]** (abt 1807–1841) came from slightly further afield — christened at Trinity Church, Ely, Cambridgeshire, in January 1808, she was the daughter of **Thomas Euseden** (1764–1842) of Ely. The Eusden family's roots were in the Ely area, a market town some fifteen miles east of Swavesey. Mary married Richard Tabraham on 3 August 1828 at **Fen Stanton** — the parish where she would spend the rest of her short life. She died around 1841 in Fen Stanton, aged about 34, when her youngest child was less than a year old.

Their children — [[ann-tabram-1829|Ann]] (1829), **Susan** (1831), **John** (1836), and **George Richard** (1840) — were born in the Fenstanton area. Ann moved to Swavesey on her marriage in 1850; the others stayed in the Fen Stanton area. Susan Tabram (1831–1911) and John Tabram (1836–1901) lived their full lives there. George Richard died young in 1857, aged seventeen, having never left.

Richard Tabraham's granddaughter [[ann-tabram-1829|Ann Tabram]]'s deliberate act of naming — giving her sixth child the middle name **Eusden** in 1865, twenty-four years after her mother Mary's death — is the most vivid evidence of how closely this cluster of families felt their connections to this ground.

## The Norman family in Swavesey: three generations

The Norman family's documented presence in the Over/Swavesey area now extends back to **[[robert-norman-1769|Robert Norman]]** (1769–1842), who was born in Over, Cambridgeshire. Robert married **[[frances-green-1769|Frances Green]]** (1769–1810) at Swavesey in November 1788; they had seven children, of whom the third was [[thomas-norman-1793|Thomas Norman (1793–1855)]]. Frances died in 1810; Robert lived on in Over until 1842.

**[[thomas-norman-1793|Thomas Norman (1793)]]** moved into Swavesey itself, marrying **[[elizabeth-fear-1794|Elizabeth Fear]]** (1794–1832) there in August 1815. They initially settled near Willingham (their first child was christened there two months after the wedding) but were back in Swavesey by the 1820s. Elizabeth died in July 1832 — probably from complications of their youngest child Ann's birth — leaving Thomas a widower with four children under twelve. He raised them alone, never remarrying, and died in Swavesey in December 1855.

**[[thomas-norman-1827|Thomas Norman]]** (1827–1905) was born in Swavesey and spent his entire life there — a quintessential figure of the settled English agricultural village. He was an **agricultural labourer** through all his working years, appearing in the Swavesey census returns from 1841 through 1891. He moved only once, and only in his late seventies: by the 1901 census he had followed his son [[tom-norman-1856|Tom]] to Alconbury Weston, Huntingdonshire, where he died in 1905 aged about 78.

He married [[ann-tabram-1829|Ann Tabram]] — herself from just across the border in Fen Stanton — on 3 February 1850 in Cambridgeshire. Both had lost their mothers young: Thomas's mother Elizabeth Fear died when he was about five; Ann's mother Mary Eusden died when she was twelve. Together they had seven children, all born in Swavesey.

## The seven Norman children: who stayed, who left

Of the seven children of Thomas and Ann, four spent most or all of their lives in or near Swavesey; two left for distant destinations; one moved within Huntingdonshire and returned.

**Those who stayed:**

- **[[alfred-eusden-norman-1865|Alfred Eusden Norman]]** (1865–1896) — born and died in Swavesey, never leaving. He died aged about 31, predeceasing both parents. His name — carrying his maternal grandmother Mary Eusden's maiden name — is itself a monument to this family's attachment to its origins.

- **[[robert-henry-norman-1868|Robert Henry Norman]]** (1868–1954) — the youngest child, and the most deeply rooted. He appears in the Swavesey census at every decennial point from 1871 through 1911. He died in Cambridgeshire in 1954 aged about 86 — the last of his generation, and the longest-lived of all Thomas and Ann's children.

- **[[mary-ann-norman-1861|Mary Ann Norman]]** (1861–1951) — documented in Swavesey from 1871 through 1939; she never left. Her husband [[george-causer-1859|George Causer]] died in 1898; by 1911 she was a widowed charwoman with two daughters still at home. By 1939 she was at **Middle Watch**, the same Swavesey hamlet where her brother Robert Henry also lived — an Old Age Pensioner with daughter Elizabeth and a lodger. She died in Cambridgeshire in 1951 aged about 89.

- **[[tom-norman-1856|Tom Norman]]** (1856–1925) — the third child, he stayed in Swavesey through 1881 but by 1891 had moved to **Alconbury Weston**, Huntingdonshire, about twenty miles north-west. He remained there through 1901, his father Thomas following him there by the same census. By 1911 Tom had moved again to **Eaton Socon** (on the Huntingdonshire/Bedfordshire border). He died in Huntingdonshire in 1925 — the most mobile of the stay-behinds, though always within the arc of Cambridgeshire and Huntingdonshire.

**Those who left:**

- **[[george-norman-1851|George Norman]]** (1851–September 1903) — the eldest, he was in Swavesey through 1871. Between 1871 and 1875 he left for **West Hartlepool, County Durham** — moving north with his brother Richard, not south to London. He married **Margaret J Garry** at Christ Church, West Hartlepool, in December 1875. He and Margaret raised ten children in Stranton and West Hartlepool. He died in Hartlepool in September 1903, aged about 51. A FamilySearch record once conflated him with a different George Norman who did go to London (Wandsworth/Croydon/Stilton), but those residences are not his.

- **[[richard-norman-1853|Richard Norman]]** (1853–?) — the second child. He and George were christened together at **Fen Stanton** on 3 July 1853 — Ann Tabram using her own home parish for the ceremony — when Richard was a newborn and George about two. Richard left Swavesey for Hartlepool around the same time as George. He married **Ann Elizabeth Randall** in Hartlepool in October 1875, just two months before George's wedding at the same town. He never returned south. He and Ann had eleven children, all raised in West Hartlepool. The pull of the industrial north — shipbuilding, iron, coal, and the port trades — drew both eldest brothers far from the flat Cambridgeshire fenlands of their birth.

- **[[charles-norman-1859|Charles Norman]]** (1859–1907) — the fourth child. He left Swavesey for London, found work as a carter hauling hay, and eventually rose to foreman at Carter Paterson's Smithfield stables. He is the only Norman sibling to have built a family and settled permanently in London. See [[charles-norman-1859]] and [[norman-birchmore-1883]].

## The christening at Fen Stanton, 1853

One detail illuminates the family's dual geography. George and Richard Norman, born 1851 and 1853, were christened together at **Fen Stanton** on 3 July 1853 — not at Swavesey, where they lived, but at the parish where their mother Ann had been born and her Tabraham family remained. This was Ann Tabram's conscious choice: to bring her first two children to her own home parish for their christening, probably when both her father Richard Tabraham and her siblings were still present. It is a rare glimpse of the family crossing the few miles between the two communities and treating them as a single extended territory.

## The wider scatter from Swavesey

By the 1890s and 1900s, the Norman family had spread across a crescent from Hartlepool in the north to London in the south, with Swavesey and Huntingdonshire as the anchoring middle:

| Person | Direction | Destination |
|---|---|---|
| Richard Norman | North | West Hartlepool, County Durham |
| George Norman | North | West Hartlepool, County Durham |
| Charles Norman | South | London (Smithfield) |
| Tom Norman | North-west | Alconbury Weston → Eaton Socon |
| Thomas Norman (father) | North-west | Alconbury Weston (followed Tom) |
| Mary Ann Norman | — | Swavesey (never left; Middle Watch 1939) |
| Alfred Eusden Norman | — | Swavesey (never left) |
| Robert Henry Norman | — | Swavesey (never left) |

The core of the family — Alfred, Robert Henry, and Mary Ann throughout her life — remained in Swavesey. The Tabraham cousins (Susan and John Tabram) remained in Fen Stanton. The three who left for cities all migrated by 1881.

## The fraternal bond: names as memorials

[[richard-norman-1853|Richard]] and [[george-norman-1851|George]] both settled in Hartlepool, living as neighbours in Stranton through the 1880s and 1890s. Their families grew up alongside each other. Richard and Ann Randall named their children in ways that echo Swavesey explicitly:

- **George Norman** (1895) — the ninth child, named for brother George, who was alive and living nearby
- **Charles Norman** — the name given to three successive sons (1878, 1880, 1900), named for brother [[charles-norman-1859|Charles]] who went to London

Richard tried the name Charles three times: the first died as an infant in 1878, the second survived to 18 before dying in 1898, and the third (born 1900) lived to 1987. The persistence is striking — Richard wanted a son named Charles and kept the name until one survived. George, for his part, named one of his own sons George (born 1889).

This is the same impulse that runs through the whole family: Ann Tabram naming her son **Alfred *Eusden* Norman** (1865) after her dead mother, the Tabraham custom of the Randall name (Richard *Randall* Norman, 1886), Charles Norman's own son carrying the name. In this family, a name given to a child is a claim that the person named is not forgotten.

George died in September 1903 in Hartlepool, aged about 51, leaving Margaret and several young children. Richard lived on, surrounded by a family that included a son named in his brother's honour.

## Agricultural character

This was emphatically agricultural country. Thomas Norman's occupation of **agricultural labourer** throughout his life reflects the landscape: flat fen-edge arable farmland, drainage channels, large fields worked by hired labour for tenant farmers. The St Ives area was a significant grain-growing district, and the agricultural communities that supplied it changed little in character between the 1840s and the 1890s. Of those who stayed, the census record is consistent: **Alfred Eusden Norman** (1865–1896) and **Robert Henry Norman** (1868–1954) are both recorded as Farm Labourer or Agricultural Labourer across their census appearances. **Tom Norman** followed the same path until his fortunes shifted upward — he appears as Farm Bailiff by 1891, a supervisory role above common labourer, and later as a beerhouse keeper at Eaton Socon. Four generations of Normans — Robert (1769), Thomas (1793), Thomas (1827), and two of Thomas's sons — all worked the same flat fen-edge farmland.

Charles Norman's path from Swavesey to London ran through this agricultural economy. He began as a carter hauling **hay from Cambridgeshire** to London — the produce of exactly this landscape being driven to feed the horses of the metropolis. The route from Swavesey to London was a route he knew.

## Open Questions

- Did any of the Tabraham cousins (Susan, John) have children who maintained the Fen Stanton family presence into the 20th century?
- George Causer's origins in Staffordshire and how he came to Swavesey remain unknown. His occupation as Engine Driver is unusual for the village — was he on the railways or on steam farm machinery?
- Robert Norman's parents are not yet identified — the Norman roots before ~1769 in Over/Swavesey remain unknown.
- Richard Tabraham's occupation in the census — if confirmed as agricultural labourer, this would close the loop between the Fen Stanton and Swavesey families as two lines of the same rural working class.
