The [[bunker-norman-1921|Bunker-Norman family]] moved to Harpenden after Ron's discharge from the Royal Navy in 1930, and lived there for forty years across three houses.

## Why Harpenden

The choice of Harpenden in 1930 was not accidental. Several factors converged to make it the right place:

**The business.** Harry Thody was based in [[luton|Luton]], where Thody Bros operated. Harpenden sits five miles south of Luton on the Midland Main Line — close enough for daily involvement in the business without living in Luton itself.

**Ron's family.** [[eliza-geary-1859|Eliza Geary]] and [[frederick-bunker-1869|Frederick Bunker]] were in Luton (Frederick died there in 1932, Eliza in 1942). [[beulah-bunker-1896|Beulah]] and [[harry-thody-1894|Harry Thody]] were at 9 Oakley Road, Luton. Harpenden kept Ron close to his mother and his sister without the families living on top of each other.

**Ivy's roots.** [[studham|Studham]] and [[whipsnade|Whipsnade]] — where Ivy had spent childhood summers at Rose Cottage with Lucy Cook, where she and Ron had been caught on their wedding day in 1921 — lie about seven miles west of Harpenden, across the Chiltern escarpment. Hertfordshire was [[ellen-birchmore-1860|Ellen Birchmore]]'s county. Settling here kept one foot in the world that had shaped Ivy's childhood.

**Not London.** By 1930, the world the Norman family had occupied was changing. Charles Norman had been dead for twenty-three years. The Hosier Lane and Smithfield world had dispersed entirely. The family had already moved once, from the City to Dalston/Kingsland. Ivy's sisters were scattering — Lillian to Salisbury, Mabel and Nellie to Clapham, others to Balham and Hackney. Ellen was still alive at De Beauvoir Road, with Norah keeping house, but the dense Norman family cluster of Ivy's childhood no longer existed as a coherent pull. Harpenden was a deliberate step away from London — not a break from family, but a choice of a different kind of life.

Harpenden itself was a prosperous market town with a strong Nonconformist tradition and a distinctly middle-class character — leafy, semi-rural, with Arts and Crafts housing stock that signalled a particular kind of respectable aspiration. It was a deliberate step up from Luton: close enough for the business, far enough to be somewhere else entirely. Ron was a business owner, not an employee; Ivy ran the books. They sent both sons to **St Albans Cathedral School** in St Albans as day pupils — a private school, five miles down the Midland Main Line. Norman and Alan cycled there and back. Alan remembered grabbing onto the backs of lorries to be hauled up the long hill into St Albans. *Source: family memory.* These were not the choices of people content to remain in the world they had come from.

**32 Park Hill** — their first Harpenden home, purchased around 1930 when the furniture business with Harry Thody was getting established. Confirmed address from the 1939 National Register. During WW2 it became a gathering point for the wider family: Ivy's sister Lillian lived there keeping house, and later Mabel, John Mason, and Nellie moved in after their Brixton shop was hit by a V1 bomb. Ron worked at de Havilland in Hatfield during the war; Ivy ran the furniture shop.

**"Journey's End"** — purchased sometime in the 1950s after both sons had left home (Norman in the early 1950s, Alan emigrated to Auckland in 1957). Not far from Park Hill. It is possible that Jacqueline Reynolds, who later married Alan, stayed here rather than at Park Hill.

**Kinsbourne Green** — a development on the northern outskirts of Harpenden, purchased around 1962. See [[kinsbourne-green]] for full detail. This was their final home in England. The development may have been a gated private estate — family memory suggests this, though it has not been confirmed from other sources. *The Harpenden History Society has a record for "Green Lawns, Kinsbourne Green" which may be the relevant development and is worth checking.* The property had a private tennis court — unusual for the period and not something Ron or Ivy, then in their sixties, used. They lived here until emigrating to Auckland in 1970, when the house was sold and their possessions shipped to New Zealand.
