Whipsnade is a small village of 928 acres on the Bedfordshire/Hertfordshire border. For this family network it is the deep ancestral home — the Cook, Birchmore, Matthews, and Bachelor families were all rooted here across several generations before the Birchmore Plummer marriages of the 1830s–1860s.

## The Village

The land rises from 500ft in the south-east to 800ft at the north, where the parish meets Dunstable Downs — nine counties visible on a clear day. Soil is clay over chalk; crops were wheat, barley, beans, and turnips. On the western edge of the parish chalk was actively quarried.

The village is arranged around a large green through which the road runs east–west. The key landmarks from the 1912 Victoria County History, all visible from the green:

- **St Mary Magdalene church** — small brick building with a 16th-century tower; pulpit and altar rails 17th-century. Three bells, including a tenor of 1630 ("God Save the King"). Parish registers survive from 1712. All the births, marriages, and deaths of the families in this wiki passed through this register.
- **The rectory** — facing the church; a brick building, part 17th century.
- **Hill Farm** — opposite the rectory.
- **The Methodist chapel** — west of Hill Farm. (Charlotte Matthews and David Cook were Methodist; GBP Sr's connection to Nonconformism is unconfirmed but possible given the community.)
- **[[dell-farm|Dell Farm]]** — reached by one road across the green. Farmed by [[james-edward-hain-1832|James Edward Hain]] (1832–1911) and then his son [[james-hain-1877|James Hain]] (1877–1932), who married [[margaret-anne-bransom-1880|Margaret Anne Bransom]] in 1905 and purchased the farm outright at the 1915 Pedley Settled Estates auction. See [[dell-farm]] for the full ownership history.
- **Hall Farm** — reached by the other road across the green.

The old names: *Wylibesende* (13th century), *Wehsnade* (14th century).

## Enclosure and Land Tenure

The parish was enclosed by Act of Parliament in **1798**. William Beckford acquired the right to enclose common lands in return for a £30 yearly rentcharge applied to the poor rates. The enclosure is the legal backdrop against which the families in this wiki farmed and laboured — the Bachelor, Batchelar, Matthews, and Cook families all appear in the agricultural records of this enclosed landscape.

## Houchen End / By Whipsnade Green

The hamlet of Houchen End (also called "By Whipsnade Green") appears repeatedly in the records: Thomas Matthews and Mary Wilson lived here; Charlotte Matthews and David Cook lived here; Ruth Cooke lived here; GBP Sr himself has an address here. This cluster of households was the physical centre of the interlinked families — a few hundred yards from the Chequers Inn and visible from the green.

## The Mormon Conversions — 1840s to 1861

The most dramatic event in Whipsnade's nineteenth-century family history has no monument and left no mark on the village itself: in the 1840s, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints reached the Matthews family at Houchen End, and the village began to divide between those who went and those who stayed.

The Church's British Mission was active in Bedfordshire by the mid-1840s. Whipsnade sits at the edge of the Chilterns, but the chalk villages here were part of the same evangelising circuit as the Bedford Plain — rural poverty, agricultural labourers with nothing to lose, and the LDS promise of a literal new world in the American West. At least three of the nine children of [[thomas-matthews-1767|Thomas Matthews]] and [[mary-wilson-1779|Mary Wilson]] converted, and all three eventually crossed the Atlantic.

### The branch chain

The institutional sequence matters for understanding which branch each family member joined. In April 1846, Elder Elisha Hildebrand Davis (president of the London Conference) baptised **Benjamin Johnson** of Northall, Buckinghamshire, at Whipsnade. Johnson became the founding convert of the area and the dominant local missionary — personally responsible for approximately fifty baptisms across several years. He and his wife Charlotte founded the **Eaton Bray Branch** (Bedfordshire) in 1846, then the **Whipsnade Branch** (Bedfordshire), which was formally established on **February 27, 1848**. When the Johnsons relocated to Northall (a hamlet of Edlesborough, Buckinghamshire), the branch followed them: on **April 1, 1849**, it was reorganised as the **Edlesborough Branch**, which grew to over 160 members at its peak and became the largest LDS congregation in nineteenth-century Buckinghamshire. The branch met at a public house called **The Good Intent**, with an adjacent pond serving as the baptismal font; the building still stands at Edlesborough, now converted to private houses.

### The conversions

With this framework in place, each Matthews family conversion can be placed:

**[[daniel-willis-matthews-1820|Daniel Willis Matthews]]** (born Whipsnade, 1820) and his wife [[sarah-garner-1822|Sarah Garner]] were baptised together on **12 September 1846** — into the Eaton Bray Branch, which Benjamin Johnson had founded just months earlier. This was before the Whipsnade Branch existed.

**[[william-matthews-1806|William Matthews]]** (born Kensworth, 1806), the eldest son still at home, followed on **5 April 1848** — into the newly established Whipsnade Branch, just five weeks after it was formally constituted.

Then, on **4 January 1851** — captured in the same census that recorded her still living at Houchen End with Thomas — **[[mary-wilson-1779|Mary Wilson]] herself was baptised** at the **Kensworth Branch**, aged about 71. LDS records spell the branch name as "Hensworth Branch," a consistent phonetic transcription variant: missionary Elder Job Smith's journal mentions visiting "the branches of Luton, Hensworth, and Eaton Bray," and a Kensworth Branch Record (film no. 86979, Family History Library) is explicitly cited in the scholarly literature on this circuit. Given the family's deep roots in Kensworth — Thomas's birthplace; the village where the family lived before moving to Whipsnade — the Kensworth Branch was the natural congregation for Mary Wilson. The mother of the family had joined her emigrating children in faith, though she never made the crossing.

### The scale of emigration

Of the 163 members on the Edlesborough Branch record, **77 (47%) can be identified as emigrants**, departing 1851–1872. Across all four documented LDS branches in Buckinghamshire (Edlesborough, Simpson, Wooburn Green, Aylesbury), 136 of 266 members (51%) emigrated — above the national average of 44% for English converts in the period 1851–1870. The Matthews family's departures were not isolated acts of faith: they were part of a near-majority movement out of a specific congregation.

**[[able-matthews-1813|Able Matthews]]** (born Whipsnade, 1813) was also in the LDS fold by the time he sailed, though the exact date of his baptism has not been found.

### The departures

The first to leave were **Daniel Willis and William** — they sailed together from Liverpool on **17 January 1853**, arriving New Orleans on **6 March 1853**. Both then joined the **Cyrus H. Wheelock Company**, which gathered converts at Keokuk, Iowa and set out overland on **13 June 1853**. They crossed the Atlantic and then the plains together.

The overland crossing was catastrophic for Daniel. His wife Sarah Garner, who had reached Iowa alive, **died on the trail in October 1853** — the crossing buried her on the Nebraska plains alongside two of their children, Sarah Ann (9) and Emma (2). Daniel arrived in Utah a widower. He settled eventually in the remote canyon country of **[[virgin-utah|Virgin City, Washington County]]**, where he farmed for fifty years and died in 1903.

William's fate after the crossing is less documented. He is confirmed in the Wheelock Company records and in LDS records, but has not been traced in Utah censuses.

**Able sailed in 1855**, with his wife [[mary-walker-1817|Mary Ann Walker]] and four children. The family stopped in **St. Louis, Missouri**, where Mary died — likely in the late 1850s. Able made the final crossing alone with his children in 1861 with the **David H. Cannon Company**, reaching Utah on 16 August 1861. He settled in **Lehi, Utah County**, remarried a Scottish woman named [[jennette-matthews|Jennette]], and died there in 1891.

### Those who stayed

The split in the family is the most humanly striking part of this story. Of the nine Matthews children, three emigrated; two others — **[[anne-matthews-1815|Anne]]** and **[[charlotte-matthews-1823|Charlotte]]** — stayed in Whipsnade all their lives. Anne had married [[george-birchmore-plummer-1813|George Birchmore Plummer Sr.]] in 1835 and was embedded in the village's social fabric; she died in 1858, the year the Utah converts were still establishing themselves. Charlotte married [[david-cook-1816|David Cook]] and never left — she lived at Houchen End until her death in 1910, nearly six decades after her brothers departed.

**Thomas the younger** stayed in Kensworth, died there in 1862. Whether he ever converted is unknown.

**Mary Wilson** was baptised but never emigrated. She was over 70 in 1851, and the overland trail to Utah — involving a sea crossing, months of riverboat travel, and a wagon trek of over 1,000 miles — was genuinely beyond the old. She outlived her sons' departures and died in England, date unknown.

The Matthews family is the clearest example in this wiki of an ordinary English village household fractured by the LDS conversion wave — the same smallholding community at Houchen End producing, within one generation, both a family that put down permanent roots in Whipsnade and Bedfordshire, and families that ended their lives in Utah's canyon desert and the salt-flat towns of the Great Basin.

## The Chequers Inn

Whipsnade's only public house, first recorded in the county licensing register of 1822, on the green at Houchen End. In the 1843 sale particulars (archive ref BH409) it was a working rural pub: bar, tap room, parlour, cellar, three bedrooms, cow house, two wood barns, and a two-acre paddock.

### Ownership chain

The pub passed through a sequence of brewery owners before eventually being rebuilt:

- **Early 1800s–1843:** Edward Burr's **Dunstable Brewery** — first recorded owner; premises auctioned 1843
- **~1843–1857:** Thomas Robert Rackstrow of Hemel Hempstead — likely the buyer at the 1843 auction
- **1857–by 1891:** Charles Meacher of Ivinghoe — Rackstrow conveyed to him in 1857; listed as owner in the 1871 licensing register
- **by 1891–1927:** **Roberts & Wilson** (Ivinghoe brewers) — acquired Meacher's business; still owner at the December 1926 valuation
- **1927:** **Benskin's Watford Brewery** — Roberts & Wilson sold their estate to Benskin's; the old building was subsequently demolished and the Chequers rebuilt, probably late 1920s or early 1930s

### Licensee sequence

The licensee register (archive ref CLP13) records a sequence of operators under those successive owners. Licensees changed far more frequently than owners.

- **1822–1823:** John Grey
- **1823–1828:** Thomas Birch; Richard Durrant; William Brandsout (exact sequence unclear in the register)
- **1843:** [[samuel-heels-1797|Samuel Heeles]] — documented tenant in the 1843 sale particulars; rent £16 p.a.; died 1845
- **1847:** [[hannah-maria-heels-1814|Mrs Hannah Maria Heels]] — Samuel's widow; held licence in her own name
- **1851–1861:** [[abraham-batchelor-1825|Abraham Batchelar]] — Hannah's second husband
- *(1861–1869: not accounted for in surviving register summaries)*
- **1869–1884:** [[george-birchmore-plummer-1813|GBP Sr]] — listed as "George Birchmore" in the register; took over from December 1867 on his marriage to Ruth Cooke (the 1869 register entry may lag the actual handover)
- **1884–1904:** [[ruth-cooke-1820|Ruth Cooke]] — listed as "Ruth Birchmore" as GBP Sr's widow; ran the inn alone until her death there on 22 May 1904
- **1904–1908:** [[charles-cook-1843|Charles Cook]] — Ruth's nephew (son of her brother [[david-cook-1816|David Cook]]); died 5 Jan 1908
- **1908–1909:** [[lois-brandsom-1843|Lois Cook (née Brandsom)]] — Charles's wife; listed as "Louis Cook" in the register (transcription error for Lois)
- **1909–1910:** James Vicars
- **1909–1911:** Alfred Beard; Albert Gibbs (dates unclear in the register for this period)
- **1911–1912:** Walter Edwards
- **1912–1925:** Charles Edwards
- **1925–1926:** Joseph Baker
- **1926–1931:** Thomas Watson — tenant under Roberts & Wilson, then under Benskin's following the 1927 sale
- **1931 onward:** later licensees under Benskin's in the rebuilt pub

### The rebuild

The old Chequers building survived into the 1910s — the modest house described in the 1843 particulars, with its basement cellar, kitchen, and three bedrooms. After Benskin's took over in 1927, they demolished and rebuilt it in a larger, more modern form, probably in the late 1920s or early 1930s. The timing roughly coincides with the ZSL's construction of Whipsnade Zoo (Hall Farm acquired 1926, zoo opened 1931), but no source directly links the pub rebuild to the zoo development.

See [[birchmore-plummer-cooke-1867]], [[chequers-inn-whipsnade-bedfordshire-archives]], and [[chequers-inn-ownership-perplexity-2026]].

## Hall Farm and Whipsnade Zoo

Hall Farm is at the **western end of the parish**, the same end of the village as the hamlet of Houchen End. This places it immediately adjacent to where [[george-birchmore-plummer-1813|GBP Sr]] lived — Hall Farm was not a distant employer but the working farm next to his hamlet. His daughter [[ellen-birchmore-1860|Ellen Birchmore]] worked there as a cook before 1883 — this is where she met [[charles-norman-1859|Charles Norman]], who hauled hay from the farm to London.

By the 1920s Hall Farm had fallen into dereliction. In **late 1926 the Zoological Society of London (ZSL) purchased the Hall Farm estate** (approximately 500–600 acres) for **£13,480**. ZSL's Secretary Sir Peter Chalmers Mitchell had first proposed a country zoological park in 1903; the Council approved the project in 1926. Construction from 1927 to 1931 employed about 150 unemployed Welsh miners under a Ministry of Labour scheme. Dr Geoffrey Vevers moved to Whipsnade in November 1930 to manage the final preparations.

**Whipsnade Park opened to the public on Saturday 23 May 1931** — just 27 years after Ruth Cooke's death at the Chequers Inn a few hundred yards away.

Opening day in rain: 1,080 visitors. Whit Sunday: 10,000. **Bank Holiday Monday: 26,946 visitors** — food and beer gone by midday, railway bookings from London suspended by police order. The *Morning Post*: "WHIPSNADE BESIEGED." The entire year's budgeted income had been £2,500; the first weekend far exceeded it.

Ellen Birchmore died in 1940 — nine years after the farm she once cooked in had become one of England's most visited attractions.

## The Tree Cathedral

In 1927 **Edmund Kell Blyth** (1898–1969) purchased Chapel Farm at the north-western edge of the parish, and from **1930** he began planting a living First World War memorial on a twenty-acre field he had used for chicken farming. The plantation follows the ground plan of a medieval cathedral — nave, transepts, chancel — and was conceived, in Blyth's own words, as "an act of faith, hope and reconciliation." The immediate inspiration was a visit to Liverpool Anglican Cathedral (then under construction); driving home through the Cotswolds he saw evening sunlight on a hillside coppice and thought "here was something more beautiful still."

Blyth's commemorated comrades were three friends lost in or because of the war: **Arthur Bailey** (2nd Lt, died 24 March 1918 in the Spring Offensive), **John Bennett** (2nd Lt, died 28 March 1918, also in the Spring Offensive), and **Francis Holland** (survived the war but died in a car crash in Canada in late 1928). Planting continued through 1939, halted during WWII, and resumed in 1947. In 1960 Blyth presented the site to the National Trust, which assumed full management in 2023.

**[[albert-bransom-1871|Albert Bransom]]** of Yew Tree Cottages was Blyth's sole helper and friend for the full nine years of planting, from 1930 to 1939. Blyth had lived at Rosemary Cottage in the village before purchasing Chapel Farm in 1927; Albert at Yew Tree Cottages — they were neighbours before they were collaborators. One source calls Albert "a friend" explicitly; no other person is named as helping Blyth during the plantation's creation. Albert's son [[frederick-bransom-1894|Private Frederick Bransom]] (2nd Battalion, Bedfordshire Regiment) had died of wounds on 1 August 1916 during the Somme campaign — the **first Whipsnade man to fall in the Great War**. Frederick's name is inscribed on the St Mary Magdalene church war memorial; his father's labour, fourteen years later, went into a different kind of memorial a few hundred yards away.

The Tree Cathedral and the church war memorial are not the same monument, but they share the same remembrance landscape. Blyth's Cathedral commemorates his own comrades; the church memorial commemorates Whipsnade's dead, among them the son of the man who helped Blyth build it.

The timing overlaps almost exactly with the Zoo's construction on Hall Farm (1927–1931): in the late 1920s and early 1930s, Whipsnade's northern edge was simultaneously acquiring two of its most distinctive features — the open-air zoo on the old farm estate, and the tree cathedral on the old chicken field — within half a mile of each other.
