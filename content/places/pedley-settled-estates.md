The Pedley Settled Estates were a large landholding across the chalk country of south Bedfordshire and the Hertfordshire border — covering Eaton Bray, Whipsnade, Totternhoe, Studham, Caddington, Markyate, and Flamstead, approximately 2,050 acres in total. The estate touches this wiki through [[dell-farm|Dell Farm]], which was part of it from 1827 until the 1915 auction at which [[james-hain-1877|James Hain]] bought it outright.

## The Pedley Family and the Settlement

The "Pedley" name comes from **John Pedley**, who acquired Dell Farm in 1827 from Isaac Dell (who had received it under the will of John Sutton, died 1795). The legal term "settled" means the estate was held under a family trust — binding future generations and restricting sale without legal process. That settlement in the Pedley name persisted even as the beneficial ownership passed to Arthur Macnamara, so the estate retained the Pedley name through to its dissolution in 1915.

## Arthur Macnamara

By 1842 the estate had passed to **Arthur Macnamara** (1831–1906). The Macnamara family already held extensive properties: Caddington Hall in Hertfordshire, an estate at Eaton Bray, and the grand **Llangoed Hall** near Llyswen in Breconshire, Wales. In 1854 Arthur married **Lady Sophia Hare**, daughter of the Earl of Listowel, at her family's estate in County Cork.

He was an ambitious builder. In the 1880s he acquired land in **Billington**, near Leighton Buzzard, constructing a Victorian manor house with stable yards, lodges, and farm buildings stamped throughout with his "AM" cypher — transforming it into a model estate village. He also attempted to reconstruct the lost medieval castle at Eaton Bray: clearing the moated site, building grandiose entrance lodges — before abandoning the project, almost certainly as his finances deteriorated.

On **11 February 1906**, Arthur Macnamara died in the Billington manor house, alone except for his housekeeper. The cause of death was cirrhosis of the liver. When his affairs were examined, he was found to be bankrupt. The Squire of Billington, who had owned a vast portion of Bedfordshire, died insolvent. Lady Sophia sold what she could, moved to Heath and Reach, Bedfordshire, and died in 1912; she was buried in Ireland.

## The Wind-Up and the 1915 Auction

A settled estate in bankruptcy is a legal tangle — trustees, creditors, and courts all have claims — and it took nine years to resolve. The First World War, beginning in 1914, added further complication. Finally, in **November 1915**, the executors put the second portion of the Pedley Settled Estates under the hammer at a London auction conducted by **Joseph Stower of Chancery Lane**, with **Arthur W. Merry** of Leighton Buzzard as land agent and Lawrence Graham & Co. as solicitors.

At that sale, sitting tenants across south Bedfordshire and Hertfordshire bought the farms and cottages they had been renting for years:

- **[[james-hain-1877|James Hain]]** purchased [[dell-farm|Dell Farm]], Whipsnade — 125 acres, 3 roods, 39 perches; farmhouse described as a "superior residence" with five bedrooms and a drawing room
- **Jeffrey Sharratt** purchased Rye Farm, Eaton Bray — 104 acres, for £2,000
- **George Brown** purchased The Old Forge, Eaton Bray — for £315

The annotated sale particulars, recording each buyer and price paid for the full 2,050 acres, are held at Bedfordshire Archives (ref Z214/3).

## Context for the Whipsnade Families

The Pedley estate was the landlord structure behind Dell Farm throughout the period when the Cook, Birchmore, Bransom, and Batchelar families lived in Whipsnade. Henry Perkins — who farmed Dell Farm from 1851 to 1871 and served as executor of [[thomas-batchelar-1803|Thomas Batchelar]]'s will — paid rent to the Macnamara estate. So did William Gladman after him, and then the Hains from 1905.

The 2,050 acres that had been accumulated by a family with an earl's daughter, a Welsh hall, and castle-building ambitions were parcelled out in 1915 to the people who actually farmed them — a transfer driven not by choice but by bankruptcy and the slow machinery of the settled estate laws.
