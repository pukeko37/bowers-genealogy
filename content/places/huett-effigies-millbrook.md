The stone effigies of **William Huett** and **Mary Huett**, husband and wife, are among the most remarkable artefacts associated with the parish of St Michael and All Angels at [[millbrook|Millbrook]], Bedfordshire. Their story spans four centuries and reaches into the Bunker family's own deep roots in the village: it was a **Bunker sexton** and his widow who were central to the effigies' rediscovery in 1919.

## William and Mary Huett

Mary Huett died in **1602**. Her husband William followed approximately twenty years later — around **1621 or 1622** (sources give slightly different figures). They were commemorated together on a **Tudor altar-tomb** in the church of St Michael and All Angels. Their carved stone effigies lay side by side on the monument: William in armour and a ruff, Mary in a gown once carved with flowers. Both heads were apparently "chopped off at the neck, possibly by Parliamentary Soldiers" during the Civil War period — damage that would later complicate the effigies' identification and recovery.

In time the effigies acquired folk nicknames. Local people called them **"The Warriors"** — or, in a variant that hints at the anxiety their displacement would later provoke, **"The Worriers."**

## The Dismantling: 1857

The effigies' original tomb was dismantled in **1857** — though one secondary source gives "the 1840s" as the date of the disruption, and the discrepancy has not been resolved against primary records. Whatever the exact year, the renovation work caused the effigies to be removed from their original position. The rector, alarmed by reports of unexplained disturbances after the tomb's removal, is said to have moved the effigies first to his cellar, then arranged for them to be buried in the churchyard.

The village subsequently reported **"unexplained cracks and groans"** emanating from the church. These sounds persisted until **1888**, when work on the church roof revealed timber devastated by **death-watch beetle** — a rational explanation that apparently put an end to the folklore of a disturbed tomb.

By the early twentieth century, the exact location of the buried effigies had been forgotten.

## The Sexton's Discovery

Before 1919, **Mr Bunker**, the Millbrook parish sexton, was digging in the churchyard and struck stone. He retrieved **a stone head** — one of the severed effigy heads — and it passed into his keeping, and then into his widow's after his death. Mr Bunker's identity has not yet been established in the records. He was from the **Edward (1742) Bunker line**, most likely descended from either **Samuel Bunker (1842)** or **Joseph Bunker (c.1837)** — the two households that constituted the entire remaining Millbrook Bunker presence from the 1860s onward. He had died before 1919.

See [[bunker-ashby-1766]] for the Edward line; see [[millbrook]] for the household-by-household picture of the Millbrook Bunkers in the Victorian and Edwardian era.

## The 1919 Rediscovery

In **1919**, the **Rector H.P. Pollard** organised a formal excavation. The Bedfordshire Archaeological Society and the Bedford Modern School Archaeology Society participated, along with a party of Bedford schoolboys. The location of the buried effigies was unknown — but **Mrs Bunker**, the aged widow of the sexton, remembered what her husband had found. She led the party to the spot in the churchyard where he had struck stone, and produced the stone head he had kept.

The excavators found the two effigies of William and Mary Huett **side by side in a shallow grave** — largely intact despite more than six decades underground. The stone head Mrs Bunker had preserved was reunited with one of the figures, and the effigies were brought back inside the church.

One source claims the head in Mrs Bunker's possession was Mary's; another implies it was William's. The britishfolklore.com account does not specify whose head it was. Given that both effigies had suffered head damage, the attribution remains uncertain.

Three contemporary newspaper articles about the event are held by a local researcher. These would be the primary sources for a fuller account.

## Current State

The effigies now rest **near the altar** of St Michael and All Angels. William wears armour and a ruff; Mary a gown once richly carved with flowers. Both are significantly damaged — the decapitation visible on each figure, the carvings worn — but identifiable and properly reunited, after more than a century of separation and loss.

## The Bunker Connection

The sexton at the time of the original discovery was a Bunker. His widow was the key informant for the 1919 recovery. This places the Bunker family, whose roots in Millbrook spanned from around 1738 to at least 1911, at the centre of a story about the village's own most ancient memorial.

The sexton role itself is significant: parish sextons were responsible for the maintenance of the churchyard, the digging of graves, and the care of the fabric. A Bunker sexton is consistent with the family's long-established presence and their deep integration into village life through the nineteenth century and into the twentieth.

## Open Questions

- Which Bunker was the sexton? He is from the Edward (1742) line — most likely Samuel (1842)'s or Joseph (c.1837)'s branch — but no record has been found to confirm the individual. Census records and parish vestry minutes for 1880–1910 may name the sexton directly.
- Whose head was the stone head? The Perplexity clipping attributes it to Mary; the britishfolklore account does not specify.
- Were the "1840s" and "1857" dates for the dismantling referring to the same event, or to two separate phases of renovation? The Bedfordshire Archives and vestry records for St Michael and All Angels may clarify.
- What happened to the three 1919 newspaper articles? The local researcher who holds them is unnamed in the sources.
