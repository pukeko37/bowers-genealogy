Between approximately 1860 and 1885, a substantial migration of families from the fen-edge villages of Cambridgeshire and Huntingdonshire relocated to **Stranton**, the inner township of West Hartlepool, County Durham. Six FamilySearch census searches across the 1871, 1881, and 1891 censuses identified approximately 120 records of individuals born in Swavesey, Fen Drayton, Fenstanton, Over, and Willingham living in the Hartlepool area. The overwhelming majority are in Stranton. This was a community migration: multiple villages, interconnected by marriage and neighbourhood, transplanting their social world into industrial Durham.

George and Richard Norman — [[charles-norman-1859|Charles Norman]]'s elder brothers — arrived in Stranton in autumn 1875 as part of this migration. They were not pioneers. They were joining a community that had been forming for at least a decade.

## The Scale

| Village | Records found | Genuine Stranton households (approx.) |
|---------|--------------|--------------------------------------|
| Swavesey, Cambridgeshire | 56 | ~25 |
| Fen Drayton, Cambridgeshire | 44 | ~15 |
| Fenstanton, Huntingdonshire | 7 | 1–2 (remainder at Hebburn Quay, Jarrow) |
| Over, Cambridgeshire | 9 | 2 (remainder false positives) |
| Willingham, Cambridgeshire | 11 | 1 (remainder false positives or outside Stranton) |
| **Total** | **~127** | **~44 households** |

These figures understate the reality. Census birthplace recording was inconsistent; many migrants gave only "Cambridgeshire" or approximate spellings. The searches returned numerous spelling variants — "Swavesay", "Swansey", "Swai?Sey", "Pendrayton", "Fenshayton" — all successfully matched. Those giving only a county or an unrecognised form were missed entirely. The true number of fenland-born residents in Stranton across these decades was certainly larger.

## The Two Main Sending Villages

**Swavesey** and **Fen Drayton** together account for 100 of the ~127 records and were broadly equal in scale. They lie four miles apart, on the same flat agricultural plain north of Cambridge, with a shared labour market and overlapping social networks. Their simultaneous contribution to Stranton is not coincidental — the two villages were already connected before migration, as the cross-village marriages below show.

**Fenstanton** (Huntingdonshire) contributed fewer residents to Stranton specifically, though Fenstanton-born individuals appear at Hebburn Quay and Jarrow — different parts of the Durham industrial belt, suggesting the Fenstanton migration dispersed more widely.

**Over, Willingham**, and other neighbouring villages contributed in smaller numbers, primarily as spouses of Swavesey or Fen Drayton migrants rather than as independent household migrations.

## Cross-Village Marriages: Evidence of a Connected World

Four couples in the Stranton records had spouses from different fenland villages — and in each case the marriage almost certainly predated the migration. These couples brought the connections between villages to Stranton with them:

| Husband | Wife | Census | Notes |
|---------|------|--------|-------|
| John Wright (Swavesey, 1842) | Elizabeth Wright (Fen Drayton, 1842) | Stranton 1871 | Children born Fen Drayton before migration |
| Joseph Middleton (Swavesey, 1848) | Mary Middleton (Fen Drayton, 1847) | Stranton 1881 | Children born across both villages |
| Thomas Payne (Swavesey, 1850) | Elizabeth Payne (Over, 1853) | Stranton 1881 | Son Ernest born Swavesey 1871 before migration |
| William Stocker (Swavesey, 1848) | Mary Ann Stocker (Over, 1848) | Stranton 1881 | Several children born Swavesey before migration |

The Wright and Middleton families are particularly revealing: in both cases children were born in **Fen Drayton** before the migration, meaning the Swavesey husband had already married into Fen Drayton before the household moved north. These villages were socially intermarrying in Cambridgeshire, and the social network they brought to Stranton was already multi-village.

## The Randall Family and the Norman Connection

The most genealogically significant finding from the Fen Drayton search concerns the **Randall family**. At least four separate Randall households from Fen Drayton were living in Stranton:

- **Richard Randal** (1834) & **Phoebe Randal** (1839) — with children Elizabeth A, Richard, John (1864), Charles (1860), all Fen Drayton born
- **John Randal** (1848) & **Mary Ann Randal** (1845) — with children Phillip, Matthew, Frederick
- **Robert Randall** (1846) & Ursula — with children Ursula, William, Hannah J, Robert
- **William Randall** (1846) & Francis — with children John, Fred, Charles
- **John Randell** (1847) — in Stranton 1871, one of the earliest Fen Drayton arrivals

The Randalls were a substantial and long-established Fen Drayton presence in Stranton from at least 1871.

[[richard-norman-1853|Richard Norman]] (born Swavesey 1853) married **Ann Elizabeth Randall** in Hartlepool in October 1875. Her birthplace is recorded as Fenstanton, Huntingdonshire — the village immediately adjacent to Fen Drayton across the county boundary. Whether she was born in Fenstanton but from a family rooted in Fen Drayton, or whether her birthplace was recorded with characteristic imprecision for adjacent parishes, is not yet established. What is clear is that the Randall family was one of the most established fenland families in Stranton, and that Richard Norman — arriving in 1875 — was marrying into a family already embedded in the community he had just joined. His marriage to Ann Randall was not a random encounter with a stranger; it was a marriage within the fenland network.

## The Early Arrivals

Both Swavesey and Fen Drayton had residents in Stranton by the 1871 census — at least four years before the Norman brothers arrived:

**From Swavesey (1871):**
- John Bowd (1842) & Anna
- John Wright (1842) & Elizabeth — cross-village family, wife from Fen Drayton
- Susan Rose (1837) & James Rose — the earliest recorded arrival, born 1837

**From Fen Drayton (1871):**
- Jeremiah Allen (1845) & Jane — still in Stranton in 1881 and 1891; a 20-year resident
- Emma Ameys (1844) & Charles
- James Lebeth (1843) & Ann Lebeth (1835)
- Samuel Spolton (1847) & Frances
- John Randell (1847) & Mary A

By 1871, Stranton already held at least a dozen households from these two villages. The migration had been running for several years before it drew in the agricultural labourers who were most affected by the depression of the 1870s. The pioneers of the 1860s likely sent information back that shaped the choices of those who followed.

## Why Stranton

Stranton was the working-class township immediately behind the Middleton Dock — the densest and cheapest-housing part of West Hartlepool. For migrants arriving without capital, it was the natural landing point. The work available to agricultural labourers — port handling, general labouring, grain-related trades — was within walking distance. And once a nucleus of fenland families had established itself, the community became self-reinforcing: housing recommendations, shared worship, familiar names, the social comfort of people who knew where you had come from. The concentration of a dozen or more households from just two villages in a single township is not explained by housing markets alone. It required active mutual knowledge.

## The Norman Brothers in Context

When [[george-norman-1851|George]] and [[richard-norman-1853|Richard Norman]] arrived in Stranton in autumn 1875, they stepped into a community that included:
- William Mole, a Swavesey neighbour
- The Benton brothers, from Swavesey
- The Brooks families, from Swavesey
- Walter Froment and Walter Sutton, from Swavesey
- Multiple Randall households from Fen Drayton
- Jeremiah Allen, from Fen Drayton — already a four-year Stranton resident

Richard's marriage to Ann Randall, a woman from the Randall family's home parish, placed him inside the established social network of the community. George's marriage to Margaret Garry, a Hartlepool-born woman, represented the first generation's integration into the local population.

## Open Questions

- **The mechanism of the migration**: who went first? Was there an employer, a contractor, or a labour recruiter connecting Swavesey and Fen Drayton to West Hartlepool specifically? Susan Rose (born 1837, in Stranton by 1871) and Jeremiah Allen (born 1845, in Stranton by 1871) arrived before the agricultural depression of the 1870s; they may have been pulled by opportunity rather than pushed by crisis. Who they knew in Hartlepool before they arrived is unknown.
- **Ann Elizabeth Randall's parentage**: she is recorded as born in Fenstanton, Huntingdonshire; the Randall family was a major Fen Drayton (Cambridgeshire) presence in Stranton. The two parishes share a boundary. Further research into the Randall family in Fen Drayton and Fenstanton before 1875 may establish whether she was part of the Stranton Randall community, and whether Richard Norman knew her before he arrived in Hartlepool.
- **Occupations across the community**: George Norman's occupations are known (grocers porter, grain porter, malster); Richard's (general labourer, cement works, Corporation). What did the wider community do — the Brookses, the Taundleys, the Randalls? Occupation data from the census images would reveal whether the Swavesey migrants concentrated in particular trades.
- **The second generation**: after 1891 the searches stop. Did the Stranton community hold together into the 1900s, or did the second generation disperse? Tracing the children — the Taundley and Brooks and Norman children born in Swavesey who grew up in Stranton — would show whether this was a transitional generation or a settled one.
- **Other fenland villages not yet searched**: Longstanton, Cottenham, Histon, Conington, Fen Drayton-adjacent parishes. The migration was probably wider than even these six villages; a broader Huntingdonshire/Cambridgeshire search would test that.
