## Key Facts

- **Source:** ZSL (Zoological Society of London) website; guest blog by Beryl Leigh (library volunteer), drawing on the ZSL Daily Occurrences archive and Annual Reports.
- **Hall Farm estate:** 500 acres on Dunstable Downs near Whipsnade. **Purchased by ZSL in 1927.**
- **ZSL Council approval:** 1926 (Sir Peter Chalmers Mitchell first proposed the idea in 1903).
- **Public opening:** Saturday 23 May 1931 (Whitsun Bank Holiday weekend).

## Construction: 1927–1931

Under the Zoological Society of London Act 1928, Whipsnade was planned as a park for breeding and exhibition of wild animals and as a British nature reserve. Dr Geoffrey Vevers (Zoo Superintendent at Regent's Park since 1923) took up residence at Whipsnade in November 1930 to oversee opening preparations.

Construction used **unemployed Welsh miners and others** under a Ministry of Labour scheme — approximately 150 men at peak, reduced to 40 by February 1931. ZSL provided accommodation and paid 25% of wages. Works included animal enclosures, public walkways, rain shelters, car park foundations, refreshment kiosks, toilets, and extensive landscaping.

Nature reserve work: 12 species of British terrestrial orchids planted in January 1931 across Wood Lawn, Cut Throat Wood, Whipsnade Wood, and the Downs. An area of chalk grassland near the White Lion has held SSSI status since the 1980s.

The Duke of Bedford (ZSL President since 1899) contributed many animals and ornamental trees: flamingos, partridges, geese, wallabies, a camel, a zebra, and others.

## Opening and Immediate Success

Captain William Beal (formerly Principal Veterinary Officer of the Gold Coast) arrived as first Whipsnade Superintendent in the first week of May 1931, signing off the Daily Occurrences from 7 May.

- **Admission:** 1 shilling adults, sixpence children; 1 shilling for car parking, sixpence motorcycles
- **Hall Farm restaurant** served hot lunches, teas, and 3-course dinners; plus a Fellows' Pavilion and four refreshment kiosks — all fully licensed
- **Opening day (23 May, dull and wet):** 1,080 visitors; £95 2s 0d at the gate
- **Whit Sunday:** 10,000 visitors
- **Bank Holiday Monday:** **26,946 visitors** — food and beer exhausted by midday; railway bookings from London suspended by police order

*Morning Post* headline: "WHIPSNADE BESIEGED. Overwhelming rush of people to the new open-air zoo. Traffic facilities break down."

The annual income estimate for all of 1931 had been just £2,500 — the Bank Holiday Monday alone likely exceeded it.

## Relevance to the Wiki

[[ellen-birchmore-1860|Ellen Birchmore]] left her job as cook at Hall Farm in 1883 to go to London and marry Charles Norman. When she left, Hall Farm was a working Chilterns farm; when she died in 1940, it had been a zoo for nine years. The transformation of her childhood workplace is part of the wider story of how rural Whipsnade changed across her lifetime.
