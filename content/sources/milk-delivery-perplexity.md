Perplexity AI compiled research on how doorstep milk delivery became standard after 1850. AI-compiled secondary research; not a primary document.

**Key points:**

- Milk was highly perishable; homes without refrigeration needed frequent delivery rather than bulk purchase.
- Doorstep delivery developed first in wealthier residential areas in London, then became widely available from mid-century as delivery technology improved.
- Standard model: horse-drawn cart on a fixed daily round; milkman delivered fresh bottles, collected empties; maintained a regular household service relationship.
- Glass bottles and returnable containers (late 19th century) made deliveries cleaner and standardised.
- In Victorian London, the milk round was the basic retail form because it matched dense housing, fast turnover of demand, and the need for a fragile daily food supply.
- Horse-drawn carts: limited range and speed; horses needed daily care; milk had to stay cool; urban traffic, narrow streets, mud, and snow created delivery challenges.

**Challenges for horse-drawn urban milk delivery:**
- Limited to a fixed radius from the dairy or depot
- Spoilage risk in hot weather on long routes
- Simultaneous management of horse, cart, containers, and customer stops
- Urban road conditions (narrow streets, mud, snow)
- Horse maintenance burden (feeding, stabling, grooming)

**Relevance to Mason and GBP Jr:**
The Mason milk shop at Hosier Lane (1874–1900) and George Birchmore Plummer Jr's milkman operation in St Pancras (1881–1911+) both operated within this doorstep delivery system. GBP Jr's occupation progression — Dairy Farmer → Milkman → Milk Carrier — exactly tracks the shift from production to retail to wage-labour delivery that this source describes. The Smithfield location of the Mason shop placed them at the wholesale/retail interface (Smithfield Market was a meat market but the area had many food suppliers).
