## Summary

A **beerhouse** was a category of licensed premises created by the **Beerhouse Act 1830** (11 Geo. 4 & 1 Will. 4, c. 64), defined legally as a place "where beer is sold to be consumed *on* the premises." Also called "small shops" or "Tom and Jerry shops."

## Key Distinctions from a Full Public House

- **Full public houses** required a licence granted by local magistrates under the Retail Brewers Act 1828, and were subject to police inspections at any time
- **Beerhouses** required only a government licence costing **two guineas per annum** (~£150 in 2010 money) — no magistrate approval needed
- The two types competed directly until the **Wine and Beerhouse Act 1869** gave magistrates authority to renew beerhouse licences (ending the laissez-faire beerhouse era)

## Relevance

Tom Norman's 1911 census occupation at Eaton Socon — **"Farm Labourer and Beerhouse Keeper"** — was a common combination. A beerhouse licence was cheap, required no premises inspection, and could be operated from a dwelling house. Running a beerhouse alongside farm work was an accessible way to supplement agricultural wages. Tom was not running a full public house; he was operating the lower-tier, easier-to-licence establishment that the 1830 Act had created.

By 1911 the beerhouse regime was already 80 years old and magistrates had had oversight since 1869. A beerhouse licence at that date required annual renewal through the local magistracy — no longer the simple two-guinea purchase of 1830, but still a distinct and more modest class of premises than a fully licensed public house.
