Background research into the operations of Carter Paterson & Co. at the period when [[charles-norman-1859|Charles Norman]] (1859–1907) worked there as a foreman horse keeper, c.1891–1907. Sources are W. J. Gordon's *The Horse World of London* (1893), the *Commercial Motor* archive (4 May 1905), and the Benjamin Hawkins Carter Paterson history site.

---

## The Company

Carter Paterson & Co. was a parcel and light goods carrier operating across London. Founded in 1860 by James Paterson (formerly of Pickfords) with the backing of Walter Carter, it started with six horse vans at George Yard, Aldermanbury. By 1887 it had become a limited liability company with Paterson as chairman. By 1893 it operated twenty depots across London and was the dominant force in the capital's parcel delivery trade. It was bought by the railway companies in 1933, merged with Pickfords in 1946, and nationalised in 1948.

The firm's livery — green vans — was so ubiquitous that the phrase "it'll come by Carter Paterson" passed into everyday speech.

---

## 128 Goswell Road: The Central Hub

128 Goswell Road, Clerkenwell (EC1) was both head office and the largest single site in the network. The original premises were a disused timber yard on which Carter Paterson took a long lease around 1870, expanding over the following decades into an adjacent depot on Central Street as the timber yard site became too crowded.

W. J. Gordon, writing in 1893, described it as functioning like a railway goods-yard: large covered sorting platforms — "banks," in the trade term — where parcels arriving on incoming vans were redistributed onto outgoing vans organised by delivery district. From this hub, Gordon reports, approximately **600 pair-horse van loads** departed daily for the suburban depots and delivery rounds.

The stabling at Goswell Road was notable for being **multi-storey** — three floors of horse stalls, one above the other, an unusual arrangement made necessary by the density and cost of land in inner Clerkenwell. Gordon observed: *"The stables are on three floors, one over the other, clean and roomy, each horse by himself, the fixed travis here taking the place of the now more customary bale."* Fixed travis meant solid wooden partitions between individual stalls — a better arrangement than the open-row design common elsewhere, giving each horse more quiet and space.

By 1890 the Goswell Road complex alone held approximately **700 horses**.

## Scale of the Horse Operation

| Date | Horses | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| c.1870 | ~6 vans | Founding stock, George Yard |
| c.1890 | ~700 | Goswell Road site only |
| 1893 | ~2,000 | Across 20 London depots |
| 1905 | ~3,000 | Peak, alongside early motor fleet |

The 1905 figure comes from the *Commercial Motor* trade press, which reported roughly 3,000 horses and 1,250 horse-drawn vans still in service alongside just four Straker steam wagons. The horse peak was therefore in the Edwardian period — right at the end of Charles Norman's working life — not at the Victorian high-water mark.

## The Depot Network

By 1893 Carter Paterson operated **twenty London depots**. Named locations include:

- **Goswell Road / Central Street, Clerkenwell** — head office and central sorting hub, largest site, multi-storey stables
- **Camberwell** — a substantial Victorian brick complex, with a faded painted legend reading "CP & COS DEPOT" surviving on the south wall; grey, cream, and red tile decoration at first-floor level
- **Chalk Farm, Chelsea, Stratford, Bermondsey** — all operational by 1870, among the earliest satellite depots
- **Brixton (Mauleverer Road)** — site acquired 1901, rebuilt 1904
- **Chiswell Street** — a separate depot occupied from the early 1880s until 1933

The network was deliberately modelled on railway operations. Parcel orders came in at "numerous order stations" — fixed collection points at shops and businesses throughout London. Single-horse vans collected from these, consolidated loads at their local depot, and sent trunk loads by pair-horse vans to the central hub at Goswell Road. Cross-town transit and final-mile delivery ran outward from there. The company achieved an error rate Gordon recorded as one misdirected package in ten thousand.

## Staffing

No precise Carter Paterson employment records survive in public sources, but estimates can be built from known figures and period norms.

**Van drivers (carmen):** The 1,250 horse-drawn vans of 1905 each required one driver, suggesting roughly **1,000–1,250 carmen** at peak. This was the largest staff category by far.

**Stablemen and horse keepers:** A commercial urban stable of this type typically required one stableman per four to six working horses (rougher than a gentleman's stable, where one groom per two or three was standard). At 2,000 horses (1893): approximately **330–500 stablemen** across the network; at 700 horses at Goswell Road alone, **115–175 stablemen** on that site.

**Foremen:** Each of the twenty depots needed supervisory staff overseeing both the stabling and the sorting platforms, plus floor foremen on multi-storey sites like Goswell Road. The foreman grade was a step above stableman — responsible for the welfare of a section of the horses, the rota of stablemen under him, and the condition of harness and equipment.

**Farriers:** A farrier could shoe approximately eight to twelve horses per day; each horse needed new shoes every four to six weeks. At 2,000 horses, this implies fifteen to twenty-five farriers in full-time employment across the network, with most kept in-house at the larger depots.

**Platform workers:** Checkers, porters, and platform hands at the sorting banks. No figures available, but 600 pair-horse van loads departing daily from Goswell Road suggests a substantial platform crew.

**Rough total workforce at the 1893 scale:** 1,600–2,300 people across all grades, of whom the majority were carmen and stablemen.

## Charles Norman's Role

Charles Norman is recorded in the 1891 census as a **horsekeeper** and in later records as a **foreman horse keeper** at the Carter Paterson stables at Goswell Road. The distinction matters: a horsekeeper fed, groomed, and maintained a section of horses; a **foreman** horse keeper supervised a team of horsekeepers, held responsibility for the condition of a larger number of animals, and was accountable to the depot manager for the running of a floor or section of the stables.

At Goswell Road, where the stables ran across three floors and held 700 horses by 1890, a foreman horse keeper's section could easily have covered 100 or more animals. He would have managed the work rota of the stablemen under him, overseen feed and bedding, dealt with illness and veterinary calls, and ensured horses were turned out sound and ready for the carmen who collected them each morning.

Carter Paterson worked its horses on a structured rotation to prevent overwork: *"one day he has two trips, the next day he has one, the next he has two, the next one"* — a rhythm a foreman horse keeper would have been responsible for maintaining across his section.

The family narrative holds that Charles met Ellen Birchmore by hauling hay from Hall Farm, Whipsnade to London. Carter Paterson's operation of 700–2,000 horses would have consumed enormous quantities of hay. Contracts for hay from rural farms — Bedfordshire being prime hay country — were a regular part of the business. A carter doing such a run would have had reason to visit the farm multiple times across a season.

Charles worked in this role until his death in 1907, aged 48, of cancer of the tongue attributed to his pipe smoking.
