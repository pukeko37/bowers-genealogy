Ronald E. Bartholomew, "The Patterns of Missionary Work and Emigration in Early Victorian Buckinghamshire, England, 1849–1878," *BYU Studies* 48, no. 1 (2009). Scholarly article based on doctoral research at the University of Buckingham, funded by BYU's Religious Studies Center. The research was prompted by Charles Dickens's account in *The Uncommercial Traveller* of 800 Mormon emigrants leaving London on the Amazon in 1863, some of whom were "platting straw" — the cottage industry of the Whipsnade area. It draws on branch membership records, missionary journals, the 1851 Census of Religious Worship, and the Mormon Immigration Index.

## Institutional Framework — the Whipsnade–Edlesborough Chain

The article establishes the direct institutional lineage connecting Whipsnade and the Matthews family to the wider LDS network:

- **April 4, 1846**: Elder Elisha Hildebrand Davis (president of the London Conference) baptised **Benjamin Johnson** of Northall, Buckinghamshire, at Whipsnade. Johnson's wife Charlotte was baptised three weeks later.
- **April–December 1846**: The Johnsons became founding members of the **Eaton Bray Branch** (Bedfordshire), just across the county border from Whipsnade. This was the branch in which **Daniel Willis Matthews** and Sarah Garner were baptised on **12 September 1846**.
- **February 27, 1848**: The **Whipsnade Branch** was formally established — the direct predecessor of the Edlesborough Branch, and the branch into which **William Matthews** was baptised on **5 April 1848**.
- **April 1, 1849**: The Whipsnade Branch was reorganised and relocated as the **Edlesborough Branch**, coinciding with the Johnsons' return to Northall (a hamlet of Edlesborough).
- **January 4, 1851**: **Mary Wilson** was baptised at the **Kensworth Branch** — recorded as "Hensworth Branch" in LDS records, a phonetic transcription variant. Footnote 41 of this article explicitly cites a "Kensworth Branch Record" (film no. 86979, Family History Library), and missionary Elder Job Smith's journal also mentions visiting "the branches of Luton, Hensworth, and Eaton Bray," confirming that "Hensworth" = Kensworth consistently in the period sources.

## Benjamin Johnson — the Founding Convert

An atypical convert: purportedly an Oxford graduate, musical, of relatively high social standing. He personally baptised approximately 50 people across the Eaton Bray, Whipsnade, and Edlesborough branches — more than any other individual in nineteenth-century Buckinghamshire. The 1851 Census of Religious Worship records him as "Benjamin Johnson, Presiding Elder, Northall Bucks." He died before the family could emigrate, leaving his wife Charlotte to raise nine children. After a decade of hardship (including being defrauded of their emigration funds by a missionary who absconded to California), the Charlotte Johnson family emigrated in 1868 after Elder Franklin D. Richards intervened on their behalf.

## Edlesborough Branch Statistics

The Edlesborough Branch (the reconstituted Whipsnade Branch) was the largest LDS congregation in nineteenth-century Buckinghamshire:

- **163 members** on the branch record
- **77 (47%)** can be identified as emigrants, departing 1851–1872
- The branch met at **The Good Intent**, a public house at Edlesborough; an adjacent pond served as the baptismal font. The building still stands, converted to two private houses, with an identifying placard.
- The branch is the only LDS congregation from Buckinghamshire listed in the 1851 Census of Religious Worship — attendance recorded as 90 (afternoon) and 100 (evening) on 30 March 1851.
- After January 5, 1850 (transfer from London Conference to Bedfordshire Conference, recorded in the *Millennial Star*), the Edlesborough Branch disappears entirely from official Church documents — the emigration wave had hollowed it out.

The national average for English converts 1851–1870 was 44%. Buckinghamshire, despite being in the less-receptive southeastern region (south of both Gay's and Fleming's north–south demarcation lines), had a higher-than-average emigration rate.

## Geographic and Social Context

Buckinghamshire was rural and non-industrial — its people were "landless labourers" or "the rural poor" rather than the urban working class that scholarship usually identifies as the primary source of Mormon converts. No apostle or Church General Authority ever laboured in or passed through Buckinghamshire. No convert from this county rose to prominence in the Church hierarchy. The pattern of growth depended entirely on local convert-missionaries: across all four branches, 65% of baptisms were performed by local converts, 16% by American missionaries, and 19% by unnamed missionaries.

**Straw-plaiting** was the major cottage industry connecting Buckinghamshire and Bedfordshire communities via Luton (the chief commercial centre, 7 miles from Edlesborough). This economic link explains the social circuit that made the LDS network transferable across county lines.
