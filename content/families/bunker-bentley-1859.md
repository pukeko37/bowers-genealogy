## Parents

[[david-bunker-1837|David Bunker]] (1837–1920) and **Marian Bentley** (born ~1831, Biggleswade, Bedfordshire — died 1914) married on **11 October 1859** at St Paul's Church, Bedford. David was 22; Marian was approximately 28 — about six years his senior. David was the fourth child of [[james-bunker-1807|James Bunker]] and [[elizabeth-watts-1810|Elizabeth Watts]] of Millbrook. Marian came from Biggleswade, a market town 15 miles east of Bedford; how the couple met is not recorded.

David died in 1920 in Peterborough, aged ~83 — the longest-lived of James and Elizabeth's nine children. Marian predeceased him by six years, dying in 1914 in Peterborough after more than 50 years of marriage.

## Children

Four children, all born in the Bedford area before the family's move northeast:

1. **Elizabeth Jane Bunker** (b. ~1860, Bedford) — present in the 1901 Peterborough household (aged 40), the last of the children still living at home by then. No marriage or death record traced.
2. **Marian Bunker** (b. ~1862, Bedford) — named for her mother. No records traced beyond early censuses.
3. **Albert Bunker** (b. ~1863, Bedford) — no records traced beyond early censuses; not the same Albert Bunker born ~1875 in New Tupton to [[george-bunker-1836|George Bunker]].
4. **Charles Bunker** (b. ~1866, Bedford) — named for his uncle [[charles-bunker-1839|Charles Bunker]] in London. No records traced.

No person pages have been created for these children. By 1901 only Elizabeth Jane remained in the household; the others had left without trace in surviving records.

## Family Life

**Bedford years (1859–~1870).** The family's early married life was in Bedford, where all four children were born. David had moved to Bedford after his father's death in 1852 along with the rest of the Bunker family, and he and Marian established their own household there through the 1860s.

**The northeast migration.** David's migration path diverged sharply from his siblings'. While George, Caleb, and Phoebe all moved southwest to Derbyshire, David moved northeast. By the **1871 census** he was in **St Ive, Huntingdonshire** — about 15 miles northeast of Bedford — working as a **Groom**, a position in domestic or agricultural service. Whether this was a move driven by work opportunity, personal connection, or simply the pull of the Great Northern Railway corridor is unknown.

**Peterborough and the railways (1881–1920).** By 1881 David had made a decisive occupational step. He was in **St John the Baptist, Peterborough**, recorded as **Wagon Builder Storekeeper (Rail)** — a combined inventory and supervisory role in the railway works. Peterborough was a major railway junction: the Great Northern Railway's main works were there, as was Midland Railway traffic. This was literate, indoor, salaried work — a significant move up from Groom, and a trajectory unlike his agricultural labourer father or his brother John's life in the metal trades. By 1891 he was **Engineers Store Keeper** — the same kind of role, confirming a settled white-collar railway career.

**A note on the 1891 census.** In 1891 David is recorded not as "Head" but as "Patient" in the relationship field, which suggests he was resident in a hospital or infirmary at census time. He recovered, however — the 1901 and 1911 censuses both record him at home in Peterborough with Marian.

**Later years.** By 1901 the household had narrowed: Marian (now 68) and David (now 63) were living with only their eldest daughter Elizabeth Jane (40). The younger three children had dispersed. In 1911 David (73) and Marian (79) are still together in Peterborough — a marriage of over 50 years. Marian died in 1914. David survived her by six years, dying in 1920 in Peterborough, probably in or near the same neighbourhood where he had lived since 1881.

**A marriage of contrasts.** Marian was six years David's senior — a relatively unusual age gap for the period, with the wife older than the husband. They had all four children quickly in the first seven years of marriage, then none after 1866. The 50-year marriage that followed was one of quiet Peterborough stability, far removed from Millbrook agricultural labour or the Derbyshire coalfield world that drew David's siblings west.
