William Thody (born about 1774, Cardington, Bedfordshire — died 7 August 1858, Chelsea, Middlesex) was a soldier of the 40th Regiment of Foot, a Waterloo veteran, and the father of [[allen-thody-1813|Allen Thody]].

## Origins

He appears in *The Inhabitants of Cardington in 1782*, listed as a 7-year-old boy at school with Mrs Howard — placing his birth around 1774. He was the son of **Samuel Thody** and **Elizabeth Pearsey**, a Cardington family. The Thody name is documented in Cardington from the mid-eighteenth century, representing at least four or five generations of continuous local presence.

## Military career

William enlisted in the 40th Regiment of Foot (the 2nd Somersetshire Regiment) at some point before 1804, when he appears in the parish register of St Clement's, Hastings — the regiment's garrison town. He is described as a bachelor. He married **Alice Gardner** there on 27 January 1804; both signed with marks. A daughter, Elizabeth Thody, was baptised at St Clement's in October 1804.

Alice presumably died sometime between 1804 and 1809. William's regiment then served in the Peninsular War, the long campaign against Napoleon in Portugal and Spain. Sometime around 1809, at **Badajoz in Spain**, he married a widow named **Elizabeth** (née unknown, formerly wife of James Meyers). She had a son, William Myers, from her first marriage, born at Elvas in Portugal. This is confirmed by a legal examination dated 5 December 1814, in which Elizabeth petitioned Cardington parish for support for her son William Myers, stating she had married her present husband "about five years ago at Badajoz."

Their son **[[allen-thody-1813|Allen Thody]]** was born 5 October 1813, in Spain — near the end of the Peninsular War, almost certainly while the regiment was still on campaign.

## Waterloo

After the Peninsular campaign, the 40th Regiment crossed to North America for the War of 1812, returning to England in 1815. William's wife Elizabeth is recorded as being in Cardington with her children in December 1814, while William was "serving in America." The regiment returned in time for the Waterloo campaign. William Thody received the **Waterloo Medal**, awarded to all ranks present at Ligny, Quatre Bras, and Waterloo (16–18 June 1815). He served under Captain J. H. Barnett and is described in his discharge papers as having been "in the action at Waterloo." He was wounded and honourably discharged.

On 19 September 1816, he was admitted as an out-pensioner of the Royal Hospital Chelsea — the institution that provided pensions to disabled and veteran soldiers. He lived on his pension for the remaining forty-two years of his life.

## Later life

William's wife Elizabeth died at some point after 1814 (exact date unknown). By 1841 he was living in Turk's Row, Chelsea — almost certainly in lodgings among other Chelsea Pensioners — recorded in the census at age 65.

In February 1845, at Holy Trinity Brompton, he married a third time: **Elizabeth Dance** (recorded in the register as "Elizabeth," though she is called **Jane** in every subsequent document). Both were widowed, both signed with marks. His father was listed as Samuel Thoady; hers as William Smith.

The 1851 census finds William, age 77, and Jane, age 60, at Chelsea, with her father James Smith also in the household. He is listed as a Pensioner. By the time of his death, they were living at **15 Paradise Walk, Chelsea**. He died there on 7 August 1858 — cause recorded as "Natural exhaustion." Jane was present. He was buried at Victoria Park Cemetery, Hackney, on 13 August 1858.

The death register gives his age as 73, but this is almost certainly an error: the 1851 census gives his age as 77, consistent with a birth year of 1774, which would have made him about 84 at death.

## Connection to the Thody family of Cardington

His son Allen Thody, born in Spain in 1813, returned to Cardington — the family's ancestral home — and lived there as a farm labourer his entire life. Father and son were separated geographically: William settled in Chelsea after his discharge, while Allen remained in Bedfordshire. Whether they maintained contact is not known. Allen's children inherited none of the story of their grandfather's soldiering: they were born into a quiet agricultural world, and Allen himself could not write.
