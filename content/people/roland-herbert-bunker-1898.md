Roland Herbert Bunker (known as Ron) was born on 11 February 1898 in **Kettering, Northamptonshire**. His middle name Herbert connects him to his uncle [[herbert-thomas-barnsley-bunker-1875|Herbert Thomas Barnsley Bunker]] (born 1875), Caleb's second son — though whether Roland was named directly for his uncle or from a wider family attachment to the name is not known. The name recurs again in Ernest's son [[herbert-arthur-bunker-1922|Herbert Arthur Bunker]] (born 1922), suggesting "Herbert" held lasting significance across the family. — not Luton, as previously recorded. Both Roland and his sister [[beulah-bunker-1896|Beulah]] were born in Kettering; the family moved to Ilford, Essex by 1901 and to Luton by 1911. He was the younger child of [[frederick-bunker-1869|Frederick Bunker]] and Eliza Geary. His sister was [[beulah-bunker-1896|Beulah]].

### The Derbyshire Bunkers

Roland's father [[frederick-bunker-1869|Frederick]] had been raised largely estranged from his own father Caleb. But Roland himself maintained contact with Caleb's second family. As a boy he used to stay with his half-uncle [[herbert-thomas-barnsley-bunker-1875|Herbert Thomas Barnsley Bunker]] and Herbert's family during school holidays — Herbert being a railway station master whose family lived in railway houses next to his stations. These visits would have been roughly between 1905 and 1913, before Roland joined the Navy. Roland also had contact with [[ernest-caleb-bunker-1882|Ernest Caleb Bunker]], Caleb's youngest son, and took his own son Alan to visit Ernest and his two sons in Bristol in the 1930s. The two branches of the Bunker family — Frederick's Luton/Kettering line and Caleb's Derbyshire line — were in contact across at least two generations, despite Frederick's distance from his father.

*Source: family oral history, from Alan Bowers (b. 1928).*

Ron joined the Royal Navy on **1 April 1914**, aged 16, as a **Boy Artificer** (apprentice Engine Room Artificer) — a peacetime enlistment, months before war broke out. His service number was **M30443**. He trained at HMS Fisgard, Portsmouth until March 1918 — four years of training before he ever went to sea.

### Naval Service Record

His rank progression through the Engine Room Artificer (ERA) grade:
- Boy Artificer: 1 April 1914 – 31 March 1918 (HMS Fisgard, training, Portsmouth)
- ERA 5th Class: 1 April 1918 – 10 February 1919 (HMS Vernon, shore establishment)
- ERA 4th Class: 11 February 1919 – 10 February 1922 (HMS Galatea; HMS Courageous; **HMS Barham**)
- ERA 3rd Class: 11 February 1922 – 10 February 1926 (HMS Vernon; HMS Victory II; HMS Furious)
- ERA 2nd Class: 11 February 1926 – 14 April 1930 (HMS Vernon; HMS Victory II; HMS Lucia; HMS Cyclops)
- **Discharged: 14 April 1930**

Character rating: "Very Good". Ability: "Superior" or "Satisfactory".

ERAs were the engine room technicians who maintained and operated ship machinery — among the highest-paid non-commissioned ratings in the Navy. The role suited Ron's mechanical aptitude and explains his later de Havilland toolmaking work perfectly.

Two of his ships were later lost in WW2: **HMS Courageous** (sunk 17 September 1939 by U-29, the first British warship lost in WW2) and **HMS Barham** (sunk 25 November 1941 by U-331 off Egypt). Ron had left the Navy nine years before either sinking.

The 1921 census catches Roland at [[studham|Studham]], visiting [[lucy-cook-1856|Lucy Cook]] on or around his wedding day, still employed by the Royal Navy and listed as serving on HMS Barham — confirmed by his service record (October 1920 – May 1923 on Barham). He continued in the Navy until 1930 — 16 years total service.

Ron married [[ivy-helen-norman-1899|Ivy Norman]] in June 1921 at Dalston, Kingsland, London, forming the [[bunker-norman-1921|Bunker-Norman family]]. They were caught in the 1921 census at [[lucy-cook-1856|Lucy Cook's]] Rose Cottage, [[studham|Studham]] in the same month as their wedding. They moved to [[portsmouth|Portsmouth]] where their sons [[norman-kenneth-bowers-1925|Norman]] (1925) and [[alan-geoffrey-bowers-1928|Alan]] (1928) were born.

After leaving the Navy in 1930, Ron went into business with [[harry-thody-1894|Harry Thody]] (his brother-in-law) in [[luton|Luton]] — Harry ran furniture removals, Ron ran the shop. The family moved to [[harpenden|Harpenden]], purchasing 32 Park Hill. When the partnership formed, they traded as **Thody Bros. Ltd** — a limited company. The name preserved Harry's brand recognition and kept "Bunker" entirely off the business, six years before the deed poll. The shop was on the corner of **Dunstable Road and Oak Road, Luton**; the Thody Bros signage is visible on the building in a photograph of one of the new pantechnicon vans.

Harry's pre-partnership business is confirmed by a separate photograph of the van, dated approximately 1928–29 by the appearance of Harry's children Robert (b. 1924) and Marguerite (b. 1926), who are present. The livery reads **H. THODY, 35 Dunstable Rd, Luton** with services listed as *Upholstery, Bedding, Curtains, Loose Covers* and, in large letters, **REMOVALS** (phone 1342). Removals was clearly the primary offering; the soft furnishings work was secondary. The formal trading name, if any, is not established from the van — the signage gives only the proprietor's name and address.

The business began in difficult circumstances. Harry's furniture-moving vehicle was a converted WW1 ambulance — known simply as "the Van" — with terminally worn engine cylinders. Ron's repair was a feat of improvisation: he bought oversize pistons and filed them oval to match the worn bores, keeping the Van running without access to reboring equipment. It was a drastic temporary solution, buying time rather than making a permanent fix. Not long after, Ron and Harry used Ron's Navy pension and savings as collateral for a substantial bank loan and purchased two pantechnicon furniture vans. A photograph of one of these — brand new, bodied by **H G Creasey & Sons** of Knebworth, Hertfordshire — the photograph was published by Creasey as an example of their coachbuilding work, and the Kensworth contact number visible in it is Creasey's own — shows the Thody Bros Ltd livery: *REMOVALS* on the front, *REMOVALS & STORAGE* and *THODY BROS. Ltd. HOUSE FURNISHERS, Dunstable Road Luton* on the side. The *Removals & Storage* wording reflects a real asset: Thody Bros operated a lockup yard and storage building at the far end of Oak Road, near Luton Town FC's Kenilworth Road ground. When the business wound up in 1970, the yard was sold to the football club, which converted it into a gymnasium. It has since been demolished as the stadium has expanded. The deal for the vans also included two black Morris 10 cars, one for each partner. Harry sold his Morris 10 immediately and bought a red Lagonda sports car instead — suggesting he had independent savings, and that the business was already profitable by this time. This expansion dates to approximately 1932 or 1933. Ron's son [[alan-geoffrey-bowers-1928|Alan]] recalls riding in the Lagonda's dickey seat on a shooting trip with Harry, probably around 1941 or 1942.

*Source: family oral history, from Andrew Bowers; photographs.*

### The Name Change

On **8 July 1936**, Ron executed a deed poll renouncing the name Roland Herbert Bunker and assuming the name **Roland Herbert Bowers**. The deed was enrolled in the Central Office of the Supreme Court of Judicature on 9 July 1936, and the statutory notice was published in *The London Gazette* on 14 July 1936 (Issue 34304). The notice gives his address as **32, Park Hill, Harpenden** and his occupation as **House Furnisher**. See [[london-gazette-1936-07-14-roland-bunker-name-change]].

Norman was 11 and Alan was 8 at the time. The headmaster announced the change to the whole school, and the boys were teased as "Bunker-Bowers" for years. The deed poll covers Roland personally; the sons subsequently appear in all records as Bowers, suggesting the name change was extended to them — either by separate enrolment (as minors, a parent could enrol on their behalf) or adopted in practice at the same time. **Ron never explained the reason to anyone**, including Ivy and his sons. His father Frederick had died four years earlier in 1932. The reason was taken to the grave.

**The theory.** We cannot know for certain. But the pattern of Ron's life makes a coherent case.

By 1936, Ron was a House Furnisher living in Harpenden — a respectable suburban town — with an expanding business and two sons at private school. His personal creed, still recited decades later to Andrew in his Auckland workshop, was "Nothing succeeds like success." The aphorism was not decorative. It described a programme.

The Bunker name carried its own history. His grandfather Caleb had bancruptcy proceedings instgated in 1880. His father Frederick was a bricklayer. Andrew recalled Alan telling him that there had been a great deal of poverty further back in the family. "Bunker" announced a working-class origin that Ron had spent twenty years systematically leaving behind. "Bowers" announced something different.

The pattern started earlier than 1936. When Ron entered the partnership in 1930, Harry had been trading as Thody Furnishers and Upholsterers. The new partnership became Thody Bros. The Thody name was kept; the Bunker name was never put on the door. There may have been a practical reason — preserving Harry's existing trade — but the effect was the same. Ron was already keeping his surname out of his business identity six years before the deed poll.

The timing is telling. Frederick died in 1932. Ron waited four years before making the change. He was evidently unwilling to act while his father was alive — an acknowledgement that Frederick would have understood it as a repudiation. The four-year gap probably reflects time needed to consolidate the business and the identity that would accompany the new name. By 1936, Thody Bros was doing well, and the boys were at the ages when a surname would shape their entire school careers.

Eliza understood exactly what the change meant. She was, by family account, very cross indeed — possibly to the point of ceasing contact with Ron. Her reaction confirms the reading. This was not a practical convenience or a business decision. It was a statement about who Ron intended to be.

His atheism follows the same pattern. Both his parents and both extended families were deeply embedded in Wesleyan nonconformist practice. Ivy attended church as well. Ron never mentioned religion — not once. The silence was conspicuous and deliberate. He did not argue; he simply removed himself from the conversation, completely. The name change was the same instinct made formal and public.

Frederick's Wesleyan conviction was not nominal. He is reported to have encountered a homeless man in Luton who had no shoes, and given him his own work boots — the boots he needed for his trade. Eliza was furious. That is the world Ron grew up in. The contrast with Ron's own priorities could hardly be starker: his father gave away necessities to strangers on Wesleyan principle; Ron arranged private schooling, a respectable address, and a new surname for his sons.

The Navy gave Ron a particular vantage point on class. As an ERA, he sat at the top of the non-commissioned ratings — the most technically skilled men aboard, trusted with the machinery that kept the ship moving. But the line between rating and officer was a social line, not a technical one. Ron had reached the ceiling available to a man of his background. He knew exactly where that ceiling was, because he had spent sixteen years looking up at it.

Norman entering the Navy as an officer was therefore not merely a son following a father. It was the ceiling broken. Ron's son did what Ron could not, and he did it on the back of the private schooling and the respectable surname Ron had arranged. That is what "Nothing succeeds like success" meant in practice.

What he was building by 1936 was legible in retrospect. One son would go on to be a Navy officer; the other would become a doctor. Ron had started as a bricklayer's son from Kettering. He ended as the founder of a prosperous business with professional sons and a different surname. The name change was a piece of the account he was giving of himself.

### WW2 and Later Life

During WW2, Ron worked at the de Havilland Aircraft Company in **Hatfield** — 9 miles from Park Hill, Harpenden, which he covered by bicycle. He was making patterns, tools and jigs for the Mosquito bomber project; a reserved occupation drawing directly on his Navy engineering skills. The factory was hit in a Luftwaffe raid on **3 October 1940**; the first Mosquito prototype flew on **25 November 1940**. Roland likely left de Havilland sometime between 1945 and 1950. Ivy ran the furniture shop throughout.

Park Hill became a wartime refuge — Ivy's sisters [[lillian-frances-norman-1893|Lillian]] and [[nellie-norman-1885|Nellie]] moved in to run the household while Roland and Ivy worked; later [[mabel-annie-norman-1883|Mabel]] and her husband [[john-james-mason-1878|Jack Mason]] joined them after their shop at 150 Acre Lane, Brixton was damaged by a V1 bomb. Thody Bros received a wartime fuel rationing exemption as a critical business.

In late 1944, **Norman Bowers joined the Royal Navy**, pursuing officer training as an Engineer — following his father's path, though as an officer.

Ron and Ivy emigrated to [[auckland|Auckland]], New Zealand in 1970 (visiting in 1968, emigrating 1970), following son Alan. Son [[norman-kenneth-bowers-1925|Norman]] stayed behind in the UK Navy. Ron died in Auckland on 31 October 1978, aged 80.

### Character and Values

Ron's character is remembered through his grandson Andrew, who spent time with him in his Auckland workshop in the 1970s. The workshop was the centre of his later life, a direct carry-over from his Navy Artificer training. Ron was a maker.

Two aphorisms recurred in that setting: "Nothing succeeds like success" and "If a job is worth doing, it's worth doing well." These were his value system, not decoration. He is sometimes described as a perfectionist. The description does not quite fit. His engineering instinct was pragmatic — fitness for purpose and longevity were the goals, not indefinite refinement. A man who had filed pistons oval to keep a van running was solving the problem in front of him, not chasing an ideal. He was comfortable finishing a job and moving on.

Ron was not religious. Ivy attended church in the earlier part of her life and occasionally with family members. Ron never mentioned religion — not once. This was conspicuous precisely because both his parents and both extended families were deeply embedded in Wesleyan nonconformist practice. Chapel was the social and moral centre of their world. Ron's silence was not an absence; it was a position.

Ron attributed the estrangement between his parents to the age gap between Frederick and Eliza. Eliza was considerably older than Frederick. The marriage was not happy, and Ron blamed the gap directly. He appears to have been sent to Derbyshire for school holidays partly as a result of this difficult domestic situation — staying with his uncle Herbert and, almost certainly, with Caleb's household too, in the years before he joined the Navy in 1914.
