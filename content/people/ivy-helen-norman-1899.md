Ivy Helen Norman was born 19 July 1899 at [[hosier-lane-city-of-london|Hosier Lane]], Smithfield, London — the eighth of nine children of [[charles-norman-1859|Charles Norman]] and [[ellen-birchmore-1860|Ellen Birchmore]]. She grew up in the [[norman-birchmore-1883|Norman-Birchmore family]].

Her father died of tongue cancer when she was eight (the JSON says eight; other sources say seven — she was born July 1899, he died September 1907). The family was supported by the older children and the extended family, including the [[mason-james-1874|Mason family]] who married into the Normans.

Ivy spent summers visiting [[lucy-cook-1856|Lucy Cook]] (her mother's step-sister) at Rose Cottage, [[studham|Studham]]. She was a good cook, learning from her mother Ellen (who had been a cook at The Chequers Inn and Hall Farm) and from "Aunt Lucy" who also grew up at the Chequers.

### Westminster Bank

At some point before the war's end, Ivy was employed as a teller at what she would have known as **London County and Westminster Bank** — the name *Westminster Bank* was only adopted in 1923, two years after she left. By the time she joined, the bank was already one of the largest in England, with a strong presence in the City. She worked there throughout the war years and until her marriage in 1921. When exactly she started is not known; she was eligible to leave school at 14 in the summer of 1913, but whether she was already at the bank when war broke out in August 1914, or whether her entry was one of the early wartime openings, is not recorded.

Women were almost entirely absent from banking before 1914 — under 1% of staff. As men enlisted, banks took on women in large numbers, and by 1921 women made up around a quarter of all banking staff. Ivy worked through the whole of that transformation.

The work required real arithmetic skill. Pre-decimal currency meant three-column calculations — pence (base 12), shillings (base 20), pounds — with non-decimal carries at each level. Tellers were expected to work quickly and accurately without mechanical aids; adding machines were only beginning to appear in banks during this period. Ledger entries were made by hand in bound books, and tellers could not leave at the end of the day until their books balanced.

Ivy was quick and methodical — not a prodigy, but she kept her books in order, rarely made mistakes, and could carry running balances for columns of pounds, shillings and pence in her head. Because her books were rarely out, she was often the first to leave. Her employment ended in June 1921 when she married — the **marriage bar** required women to leave immediately upon marriage, and persisted in UK banking until the 1950s.

She genuinely enjoyed working at the bank and was sorry when that chapter of her life closed. *Source: family memory.*

This same facility with figures served her well for forty years as bookkeeper for Thody Bros. Ron used to enjoy showing it off as aneducational party trick - asking her to add up a list of figures while the grandchildren watched. *Source: family memory; general historical context from banking histories.*

### Marriage and Family

Ivy met [[roland-herbert-bunker-1898|Roland Bunker]] (Ron) through the Thody connection: Lucy Cook's son [[harry-thody-1894|Harry]] was friends with Ron; Harry married Ron's sister [[beulah-bunker-1896|Beulah]] in 1920; Ivy and Ron married in June 1921 at Dalston, forming the [[bunker-norman-1921|Bunker-Norman family]].

They stayed at Rose Cottage, Studham before moving to [[portsmouth|Portsmouth]] (Ron's Navy posting). Sons [[norman-kenneth-bowers-1925|Norman]] (1925) and [[alan-geoffrey-bowers-1928|Alan]] (1928) were born there.

After Ron's discharge in 1930, the family moved to [[harpenden|Harpenden]], purchasing **32 Park Hill** around the time the furniture business with Harry Thody was established. This is confirmed by the 1939 National Register. They owned three houses in Harpenden over forty years: first Park Hill (~1930s–early 1950s), then **"Journey's End"** (purchased in the 1950s after both sons had left home, not far from Park Hill - Jacqueline Reynolds may have stayed here rather than Park Hill), and finally a new house in the **Kinsbourne Green** development on the northern outskirts of Harpenden (~1962, before 1964). Ron ran the Thody Bros furniture shop; Ivy managed the business books for the life of the business.

### WW2

During WW2, Ron worked at de Havilland in Hatfield. Ivy ran the furniture shop. Sister [[lillian-frances-norman-1893|Lillian]] lived at Park Hill and kept house. Ivy loved the independence of this period of her life. Later, sister [[mabel-annie-norman-1883|Mabel]], brother-in-law John Mason, and sister [[nellie-norman-1885|Nellie]] moved in after their shop at 150 Acre Lane, Brixton was damaged by V-weapons.

### New Zealand

In 1968, Ivy (68) and Ron (70) visited [[alan-geoffrey-bowers-1928|Alan]] and his family in Auckland. The visit prompted them to decide to retire to New Zealand. They returned to England and wound down the Thody Bros furniture business with [[harry-thody-1894|Harry Thody]], then sold their house in Kinsborne Green, Harpenden. In 1970 they emigrated to [[auckland|Auckland]], shipping their possessions. The FamilySearch record shows an "Immigration 1968" entry — this reflects the 1968 visit, not the emigration itself.

Ron died on 31 October 1978. Ivy was widowed for sixteen years. She died in Auckland on 25 May 1994, aged 94.

### The Photographs

After Ron died, Ivy went through all their photographs and burned them. The entire photographic record of their shared life — their marriage, their sons' childhoods, fifty years together — was destroyed.

The act is not without precedent. The destruction of a spouse's photographs and personal papers by a surviving widow is documented often enough to constitute a recognisable pattern, and archivists have a specific lament for it. It appears across cultures and periods. But knowing it is not unusual does not fully explain it.

The explanations that fit this kind of act are not mutually exclusive. Grief management is the most common: the photographs are unbearable to look at, and burning forecloses the possibility of looking later, at a weaker moment. Privacy is another: Ivy was of a generation that treated the inner life as private, and the photographs belonged to the marriage — when the marriage ended, so did the right of others to access it. And then there is the complexity of feeling that accumulates across a long marriage: photographs flatten fifty years into apparent happiness, which can feel false when the reality was more complicated.

The Ivy-specific reading is harder to frame but worth attempting. Ron spent his adult life systematically removing things: his name, his religion, his class origins. He never explained any of it - not to his sons, not, it seems, to Ivy. She spent fifty seven years married to a man who controlled how he was seen and remembered, and who kept his inner life largely to himself. She managed the Thody Bros books, held the family network together, opened the house to her sisters during the war, absorbed the name change without recorded complaint.

When Ron died, the photographs were Ivy's to decide. The burning may have been her equivalent act - the one moment she exercised control over the record of their shared life as completely as Ron had exercised it over his own identity. Not absorbed from him as a habit, but the same instinct: to decide, deliberately and finally, what would and would not be carried forward.

There is also the simpler possibility that she was protecting something - not a secret exactly, but the private texture of a long marriage between complicated people, which she felt was not for her sons or grandchildren to see or judge.

*Source: family oral history, from Andrew Bowers. The interpretation is inferred.*

An excellent cook, methodical with figures, a woman who held the family network together across fifty years of marriage and sixteen years of widowhood. Ron made the major decisions — the name change, the emigration, the shape of their life — without explanation and largely without consultation. Ivy's authority was real but operated in a different register: the household, the books, the family connections. She travelled from Victorian London to 1990s Auckland, through both world wars, and outlived him by sixteen years.
