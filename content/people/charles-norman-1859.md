Charles Norman was born 28 April 1859 in Swavesey, Cambridgeshire — the fourth of seven children of [[thomas-norman-1827|Thomas Norman]] (1827–1905) and [[ann-tabram-1829|Ann Tabram]] (1829–1897) in the [[norman-tabram-1850|Norman-Tabram family]]. His older siblings were [[george-norman-1851|George]] (1851), [[richard-norman-1853|Richard]] (1853), and [[tom-norman-1856|Tom]] (1856); his younger siblings were [[mary-ann-norman-1861|Mary Ann]] (1861), [[alfred-eusden-norman-1865|Alfred Eusden]] (1865), and [[robert-henry-norman-1868|Robert Henry]] (1868). His father was an agricultural labourer who lived in Swavesey his entire life.

Charles left Swavesey for London — part of the Victorian rural-to-urban migration. He found work with Carter Paterson & Co., the dominant parcel and light goods carrier in London, and began as a carman and horsekeeper. Carter Paterson's green vans were ubiquitous on the London streets; by the time Charles joined, the company operated several hundred horses across multiple depots and was growing rapidly.

He met [[ellen-birchmore-1860|Ellen Birchmore]] when hauling hay to London from Hall Farm, [[whipsnade|Whipsnade]], where Ellen was employed as a cook. Carter Paterson's appetite for fodder was enormous — the firm would reach 700 horses at its Goswell Road stables alone by 1890, and 2,000 horses across its twenty London depots by 1893. Regular contracts for hay from Bedfordshire farms were a routine part of that supply chain. Charles married Ellen in 1883 in Kensington, forming the [[norman-birchmore-1883|Norman-Birchmore family]].

### Residences and Career

- **1859–1871:** Swavesey, Cambridgeshire (census 1861, 1871)
- **1883–c.1889:** Islington, London — horsekeeper, Carter Paterson; Mabel (1883), Thomas George (1886), and Elizabeth Ann (1888) all born Islington
- **c.1889–1891:** [[hackney|Hackney]], London — Alice (1890) born Hackney; 1891 census confirms Hackney. No Carter Paterson depot has been identified in Hackney; he likely commuted to the main yard at Goswell Road, under three miles away
- **1891–1907:** [[hosier-lane-city-of-london|Hosier Lane]], City of London — promoted to foreman horse keeper; the family moved to be close to his workplace at the Carter Paterson stables at 128 Goswell Road, Clerkenwell, a short walk from Hosier Lane. Census 1901: City of London.

### Carter Paterson: What the Work Meant

The Goswell Road complex was Carter Paterson's head office, central sorting hub, and largest stabling site — a multi-storey yard with horses stabled on three floors, each horse in its own stall separated by fixed wooden partitions. By 1890 the site held around 700 horses; by 1893 the company operated roughly 2,000 horses across twenty London depots, peaking at about 3,000 by 1905. Charles worked at the high-water mark of the urban horse trade.

A **horsekeeper** fed, groomed, and maintained a section of horses — responsible for their daily condition and readiness. A **foreman** horse keeper ran a team of horsekeepers, overseeing a larger section of the stables, managing rotas, supervising feed and bedding, dealing with illness and veterinary calls, and ensuring horses were sound and harnessed for the carmen who collected them each morning. At Goswell Road's scale, a foreman's section could easily cover a hundred animals or more. It was skilled, supervisory work — a meaningful step above the carmen and stable hands beneath him.

Carter Paterson worked its horses on a structured rotation to prevent overwork: one day two trips, the next day one, then two, then one — a rhythm the foreman horse keeper was responsible for maintaining across his section.

The move from Hackney to Hosier Lane in 1891 almost certainly marks the promotion. Horsekeepers could live at a distance and commute; a foreman was expected to be on hand for early starts, sick animals, and the daily machinery of a yard this size.

Charles always had a pipe in his mouth. He died on 1 September 1907, aged 48, of cancer of the tongue. He left [[ellen-birchmore-1860|Ellen]] widowed with nine children aged 5 to 24.
